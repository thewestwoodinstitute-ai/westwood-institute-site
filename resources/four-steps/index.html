Dr. Jeffrey Schwartz's Four Steps for OCD

Principles from Brain Lock that explain mindful relabeling, reattribution, refocusing, and revaluing as part of OCD self-treatment education.

A Cognitive-Biobehavioral Approach

Dr. Schwartz's Four Steps help patients recognize obsessive thoughts and compulsive urges as symptoms of OCD rather than meaningful signals that must be obeyed.

The method can support exposure and response prevention by helping patients identify OCD symptoms, resist compulsive responses, shift attention, and lower the value placed on intrusive thoughts.

Quick Summary of the Four Steps

  1. Relabel: Recognize intrusive thoughts and urges as obsessions and compulsions caused by OCD.
  2. Reattribute: Remind yourself, "It's not me; it's my OCD," and understand the intensity as part of the disorder.
  3. Refocus: Redirect attention to a constructive behavior, even for a few minutes, instead of doing the compulsion.
  4. Revalue: Treat OCD thoughts as false brain messages and give them less importance over time.

How the Steps Work

Step 1: Relabel

The first step is to identify intrusive thoughts and urges as OCD symptoms. Instead of treating the thought as real danger, the patient practices naming it clearly: this is an obsession, and this urge is a compulsion.

Step 2: Reattribute

Reattribution helps patients separate themselves from OCD. The distress may feel urgent, but the intensity is understood as a brain-based symptom rather than a true need to perform a ritual.

Step 3: Refocus

Refocusing means choosing a constructive alternative activity and delaying or resisting the compulsion. The Fifteen-Minute Rule encourages patients to postpone the ritual while doing something useful, active, or meaningful.

Step 4: Revalue

With repeated practice, patients learn to place less weight on obsessive thoughts and compulsive urges. Revaluing helps the thought feel less significant, less authoritative, and less deserving of attention.

Practice Tools

  • The Fifteen-Minute Rule: Delay the ritual and observe whether the urge changes while attention is redirected.
  • It's What You Do That Counts: Progress is built by choosing a healthier behavior rather than trying to force the thought away.
  • Journaling: Writing down resisted urges and successful refocusing can make progress easier to see.
  • Anticipate and Accept: Expect OCD thoughts to appear, accept their presence, and continue practicing the response plan.

Clinical Note

This resource is educational and is not a substitute for diagnosis or treatment by a qualified mental health professional. When OCD symptoms are severe or impairing, professional treatment is recommended.

Source: Brain Lock, by Jeffrey M. Schwartz, Regan Books, 1996. More information about related books.
